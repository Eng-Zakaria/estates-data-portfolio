# Facilitator notes

Read these ten minutes before you deliver the [workshop](workshop.md). They are the things that go wrong, and what to do about them.

## Setup that prevents most problems

1. **Test the screen.** Open every report on the room's screen, not your laptop. Check the small-profile bookmark is selected so the data is small enough to read from the back.
2. **Write the as-of date on a whiteboard** and leave it there. People will quote numbers without it all session unless it is visible.
3. **Open the glossary and the registry in tabs.** When someone asks "what does that mean", you should be able to click, not explain from memory.
4. **Test that filters work.** A dead slicer in front of a room destroys the session's credibility faster than anything else.

## The three questions you will always be asked

**"Is this number right?"**
Answer: "I do not know, and neither does anyone in this room until we check. Here is how we check: the definition in the registry, the validation query that recomputes it, and the quality score. The data is synthetic anyway, so the question here is about method."

**"Can I use this for a real decision?"**
Answer: "No, the numbers are synthetic. The method transfers: the definitions, the owners, the quality checks and the review cycle. Bring your own data question to the steward and we will build it properly."

**"Who do I complain to?"**
Answer: point at the roles page. Owner for the meaning, steward for day to day, custodian for the pipeline. If you do not know which, the steward routes it. That is the point of the role.

## The two moments when the room goes quiet

**After "a blank is not a zero".** Someone will push back with "but surely empty rooms are the point?". Let the argument happen; it is the most useful minute of the session. Land it: a blank means the room had no bookings and no cover, so we do not know whether it is underused or simply unmeasured. Those need different actions, and only the second one is a data problem.

**After "a score measures the checks we wrote".** The room may hear this as "so quality scores are meaningless". Land the other side: the score is not meaningless, it is directional. It tells you whether this run is worse than the last one, and where to look first. It is a search tool, not a certificate.

## Facilitation moves that work

- Ask "what did you see that you did not expect?" It gets people looking at the screen instead of at you.
- Make the debrief short and the exercises silent. Talking first, thinking second, inverts what works.
- When an answer is wrong, ask what the number would need to be for their claim to hold. That converts a debate into a check.
- Write the room's misreadings on a list and leave it visible. People remember their own words.

## Things not to do

- Do not fill every silence. Count to ten before you rescue the room.
- Do not defend the dashboard. It is synthetic and imperfect, and saying so buys you more credibility than any number.
- Do not promise a figure to a senior manager in the room. "I will raise that as a request today" is enough, and it must be true.
- Do not skip the data protection slide because nobody objected. The slide is the reason nobody objects next time.

## After the session

1. Collect the [feedback forms](feedback-form.md) and read the free text before you write the next session.
2. Log every question you could not answer as a request through the [data request process](../process/data-request.md). A workshop that generates unlogged questions teaches people that asking is pointless.
3. Two weeks later, ask one question: did anyone quote the definition and the date when they used a number? That is the whole point of the session.

## If you are running it remotely

- Pairs in breakout rooms for each exercise; keep exercises under six minutes in the room.
- Ask the debrief questions in chat, one at a time, and read answers aloud. It stops the two silent people from being invisible.
- Share your screen for the dashboard segments only. The rest is talk.
